GRASP: Claim-Level Hallucination Detection from Attention

Project report — BCSE306L Artificial Intelligence, Topic 16: Uncertainty-Aware Abstention in LLM Applications

Method: GRASP (GRounding, Attention Sink, disPersion), version 2.

Benchmark: MUCH (arXiv 2511.17081): 4,873 generations, 20,751 claims, 4 languages, 4 open LLMs.

Main result: On MUCH's human-annotated test split, GRASP-v2 reaches 0.924 ROC-AUC vs 0.770 for CCP, the best published baseline (Δ +0.154, 95% CI +0.110 to +0.199). It wins on all four models, needs no NLI model, and costs 34–38% of generation time on a T4.

Main caveat: GRASP-v2 is supervised (trained on MUCH's training labels). The training-free variant ties CCP but does not beat it.

Report date: 17 October 2026. Supersedes the earlier report, whose headline numbers (0.8343 vs 0.8251, “6.4% overhead”, German “win”) did not survive the audit — see §8.

1. Summary

  • The problem: LLMs state invented facts as fluently as true ones. A useful detector has to score each claim, not the whole answer, so a system can flag or withhold exactly the wrong part.
  • The idea: read where the model was looking while it wrote each claim. Three attention statistics — grounding, sink leakage and dispersion — come from one extra forward pass. GRASP-v2 combines them with the token probabilities the model already produced.
  • The result: a small gradient-boosted classifier on these features beats CCP by a wide margin, on every model, and also improves selective answering: withholding the riskiest 30% of claims cuts the hallucination rate of what remains from 26.4% to 7.2% (CCP: 14.7%).
  • The honest limits: the winning detector needs labelled training data. The test split has now been read twice (v1 and v2). The overhead comparison with CCP crosses hardware. All three are stated in §8.

2. The problem

A language model always answers. Asked for a biography, it may write six fluent sentences of which one is invented, and the invented one carries the same confidence as the rest. Scoring the whole answer (“this paragraph is 70% reliable”) does not say which sentence to check. What is needed is a score per atomic claim:

“Marie Curie was born in Warsaw in 1899 and received two Nobel prizes.”

  • Marie Curie was born in Warsaw → low risk
  • She was born in 1899 → high risk (she was born in 1867)
  • She received two Nobel prizes → low risk

MUCH provides exactly this setting: claims are pre-segmented, labelled and evaluated under a fixed protocol. Its authors report that the best existing method, CCP, reaches 0.772 ROC-AUC. That is useful but far from solved, and CCP spends more compute than generation itself did.

The closest prior work is UHeads / LUH (Shelmanov et al., EMNLP 2025). It is also supervised and claim-level, and also uses attention plus token probabilities, but it trains a transformer head on attention maps. GRASP-v2 uses three interpretable statistics and a small tree model instead. A re-run of the prior-art search (October 2026, docs/03_novelty_recheck.md) found no published method that beats CCP on MUCH. GRASP-v2 has not yet been compared head-to-head with UHeads.

4. Method

4.1 Three per-token attention signals

The generated tokens are replayed through the model once (teacher forcing, no sampling). For each generated token, each kept head gives:

  • G — grounding ratio: the share of non-sink attention that goes back to the prompt rather than to the model's own freshly written text. Low grounding means the model is building on its own output instead of retrieving.
  • S — sink leakage: attention mass dumped on the first token, the transformer's “nothing relevant here” position.
  • P — dispersion: normalised entropy of the attention row. Peaked attention means one clear source; flat attention means none.

The audit fixed two implementation details. The attention row is now the one that generated each token, not the one after it. Token saliency is normalised by the number of later tokens exactly once, not twice.

4.2 Choosing heads without labels

A model has hundreds of heads. For each layer, GRASP keeps the heads whose grounding ratio varies most across tokens on 200 unlabelled generations. A head that always returns the same value cannot tell anything apart. v1 kept one head per layer; v2 keeps four.

4.3 From tokens to claims (GRASP-v2)

Each claim becomes one row of features:

  1. Attention statistics: layers are grouped into 8 relative-depth bins, so models of different depth share one feature space. For every bin, kept head and signal, the claim's content tokens give mean, max, min and a saliency-weighted mean (384 features). Each value is z-scored per (layer, head) on unlabelled data from the same model.
  2. Token probabilities (7 features): surprisal of the chosen token, entropy, top-1 probability and the top-1/top-2 gap. They come from the top-24 probabilities MUCH ships with every generation, so no extra model runs.
  3. Claim length (2 features).

A gradient-boosted tree classifier (31 leaves, 800 rounds) maps the 393 features to a hallucination probability. A training-free variant, GRASP-v2-free, uses no labels: it averages the per-model rank of mid-depth minimum grounding (sign fixed in advance) with the rank of token surprisal and entropy.

The original training-free score, GRASP-0 (saliency-weighted soft-max over a hand-weighted risk, with a claim-boundary reset), is kept as a reference row.

5. Evaluation protocol

  • Data: all 8 MUCH generation configs (Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-3-4B, Ministral-8B, each at T = 0.7 and 1.0). Features extracted for all 4,873 generations on Kaggle (2× Tesla T4).
  • Split: MUCH's own. Test is the 200 human-annotated generations (867 claims, 229 hallucinated); train is the other 4,673 (19,884 claims). MUCH stores no split field; human annotations are identified by their annotator keys (an0, an1), which gives exactly 200.
  • MUCH protocol: −1 is the hallucination class; the trailing EOS chunk is dropped; stopwords are masked for the probability baselines; only claims where GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 agree are scored.
  • Selection: every choice (feature groups, normalisation, learner, number of heads) was made by 5-fold cross-validation on train, grouped by generation, ranked by the mean of per-model ROC-AUCs. Pooled ROC could be inflated by recognising which model wrote a claim, because hallucination rates differ (Gemma ~54% vs Llama ~22%).
  • Test reads: the selection was frozen to a file before the test split was read. This was the second read; the first was v1 (§6.6).

7. Topic 16: uncertainty-aware abstention

A detector earns its keep when the system can withhold the claims it doubts. Ranking test claims by risk and withholding the riskiest share gives the following:

WithholdCCP: hallucinations in kept claimsGRASP-v2-freeGRASP-v2
nothing26.4%26.4%26.4%
riskiest 10%20.5%23.1%18.9%

riskiest 30%14.7%16.0%7.2%
Area under risk–coverage (lower is better)0.1520.1270.062

In practice, a system that withholds or routes for verification the 30% of claims GRASP-v2 rates riskiest keeps 70% of its claims at a 7.2% hallucination rate, against 14.7% with CCP. Because scoring is one forward pass plus a tree model, this works at claim granularity with modest latency.

8. Limits and corrections

  1. Supervision. GRASP-v2 is trained on MUCH's training labels (GPT-4o annotations). The fully training-free GRASP-v2-free ties CCP on ROC (Δ −0.006, CI includes zero) but is weaker on PR and TPR.
  2. Two test reads. v1 and v2 both read the test split. All v2 choices were frozen on train first, and the v2 margin is well clear of v1's, but an independent confirmation is still owed. A RAGTruth evaluation is in progress (§9).
  3. Labels. Test claims are scored against GPT-4o labels on the human-annotated samples, as MUCH's evaluator does, not against the human labels.
  4. Optimism. Test ROC (0.924) sits above train cross-validation (~0.885–0.897). The final model trains on 100% of train and the test set is small, so expect some regression on new data. The lower CI bound is 0.896.
  5. Cost comparison. 34–38% is measured on a T4; CCP's 124% is from the MUCH paper on an A100.
  6. Prior work. No head-to-head comparison with UHeads/LUH yet.

Corrections to the earlier report. An independent audit (audit_report.md) found the earlier evaluation had no train/test separation, selected the fusion aggregator on test data, compared a single-model German subset against CCP's 4-model pooled score, used attention rows shifted by one token, normalised saliency twice, and printed the soft-min formula with inverted signs. It also claimed 1–3% overhead against an estimated, not measured, denominator. All of these are fixed, and the numbers above replace the earlier 0.8343 vs 0.8251, “6.4% overhead” and “beats CCP on German” claims, which should not be cited.
